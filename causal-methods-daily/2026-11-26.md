## 1. Loperamide and survival in Ebola virus disease: an instrumental variable analysis of pooled individual patient data from the 2013-16 west African epidemic — Score 5/6

- **Journal**: Lancet Glob Health
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-25
- **Authors**: Hufstedler H et al.
- **Type**: applied causal analysis
- **Aims**: Estimate the effect of loperamide on 30-day survival in Ebola patients with diarrhoea using an instrumental variable design.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Pooled observational IPD, IDDO Ebola platform; 5509 laboratory-confirmed patients with diarrhoea, 20 treatment units, Guinea/Liberia/Sierra Leone 2014-16.
  - **Estimand / target**: Time-varying hazard ratio of loperamide receipt on 30-day mortality.
  - **Identification**: Lagged site-level prescribing preference as instrument (relevance, exclusion restriction, independence assumed).
  - **Estimator / model**: Extended Cox models within two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI), spline-based time-varying effects.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Spline specification robustness checks and negative-control exposure falsification tests.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Baseline HR 0.39 (95% CI 0.26-0.58) at day 0.5, HR 0.34 (0.23-0.51) at day 1; effect persisted through 30 days.
- **Strengths**: Preference-based IV with negative-control falsification addresses confounding by indication in a setting where trials were infeasible.
- **Limitations**: Exclusion restriction for site-level preference is untestable; retrospective field data with likely misclassified exposure.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Pair preference-based IV with time-varying 2SRI and negative controls when trials are infeasible and effects are time-dependent.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1016/j.langlo.2026.104109 / PMID:43085375

## 2. Group sequential designs and a sample size reestimation approach for partially nested trials — Score 5/6

- **Journal**: BMC Med Res Methodol
- **Pub date**: 2026-08-15
- **Authors**: Lee KM et al.
- **Type**: simulation
- **Aims**: Assess whether standard group sequential and sample size reestimation frameworks work for partially nested (clustered-in-one-arm) trials.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Proof-of-concept simulations of individually randomized trials with clustering only in the intervention arm; two recruitment strategies at interim.
  - **Estimand / target**: Treatment effect with type I error and power operating characteristics.
  - **Identification**: Randomization; heteroscedastic mixed model for arm-specific variance.
  - **Estimator / model**: Standard stopping boundaries with inflation by group sequential factor; interim variance-based reestimation of number of clusters.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Simulation across recruitment strategies; type I error, power and early-stopping probability.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Most designs met power; type I error near nominal. Enrolling all clusters upfront raised early efficacy stopping.
- **Strengths**: Fills a gap for adaptive designs in partially nested settings with practical recruitment comparisons.
- **Limitations**: Simulation-only; conventional estimates and CIs after stopping may be biased.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Plan partially nested adaptive trials by simulation and check recruitment strategy effect on early stopping.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-02969-z / PMID:43073803

## 3. Exclusion of patients experiencing homelessness from Canadian linked health administrative data research: A critical investigation — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: J Clin Epidemiol
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-25
- **Authors**: Richard L et al.
- **Type**: applied causal analysis
- **Aims**: Quantify differential invalid health-card linkage for homeless patients and its potential for selection bias.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Population-level ED visits 2022-23 in Ontario, Alberta, Yukon; 100,082 PEH and 6,838,899 PNEH visits.
  - **Estimand / target**: Adjusted incidence rate ratio of invalid health card number (PEH vs non-PEH).
  - **Identification**: Covariate adjustment; descriptive, not causal; non-random exclusion assessed.
  - **Estimator / model**: Poisson GLM with robust variance; subgroup ratios of IRRs via seemingly unrelated estimation.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Compared disparity estimates with and without restriction to valid-card visits.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Invalid HCN 9.0% vs 2.2%; aIRR 3.38 (3.31-3.45). Restriction overestimated some disparities, with modest net effect.
- **Strengths**: Very large population sample and quantification of linkage-driven selection bias.
- **Limitations**: Homelessness identified via ICD codes, likely under-ascertained; no formal bias correction.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Audit linkage-identifier missingness by subgroup before analysing linked administrative data on marginalized groups.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112597 / PMID:43085703

## 4. Prediction intervals for early outbreak models — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: BMC Med Res Methodol
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-17
- **Authors**: Kück F et al.
- **Type**: simulation
- **Aims**: Compare count-model prediction intervals for new infections during early outbreak phases.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Phase-specific comparison of exponential and Gompertz growth models for early outbreak case counts.
  - **Estimand / target**: Prediction intervals for new infection counts; coverage and width.
  - **Identification**: Not applicable (forecasting, not causal).
  - **Estimator / model**: Gaussian nonlinear least squares, Poisson and negative binomial regression; improved parametric bootstrap intervals.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Coverage probability versus nominal level and interval width.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Ignoring overdispersion causes coverage to deviate considerably from nominal; first parametric bootstrap for Gompertz mean.
- **Strengths**: Simple models usable with limited data, with explicit coverage assessment.
- **Limitations**: Simple growth functions ignore reporting delays and interventions.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Use negative binomial rather than Poisson for early outbreak forecasts to achieve calibrated prediction intervals.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-03064-z / PMID:43079301

## 5. Buprenorphine Use and State-Level Prior Authorization Changes in Medicaid — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: JAMA Netw Open
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-02
- **Authors**: Nguyen TD et al.
- **Type**: applied causal analysis
- **Aims**: Evaluate whether state laws limiting prior authorization for opioid use disorder medications changed buprenorphine use.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Repeated cross-sectional staggered policy study, IQVIA prescriptions 2014-2020, 13 treated and 25 control states, Medicaid ages 15-64.
  - **Estimand / target**: Average effect of laws on dispensing rate, initiation rate and dispensing gap, overall and by baseline rejection rate.
  - **Identification**: Difference-in-differences; parallel trends between treated and control states.
  - **Estimator / model**: Difference-in-differences regression with state-level treatment timing, stratified by baseline rejection rates.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Subgroup analyses by high versus low baseline prior authorization rejection.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: No overall change (dispensing +0.11/1000, 95% CI -0.30 to 0.52). High-rejection states: +0.29/1000 (0.07-0.51).
- **Strengths**: Large claims-based data and effect heterogeneity by policy dose.
- **Limitations**: Estimator details for staggered adoption not reported in abstract; parallel trends untestable.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Stratify policy DiD analyses by baseline exposure intensity, since pooled effects can mask real effects.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.45918 / PMID:43077204
