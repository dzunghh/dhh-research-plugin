## 1. Improved inverse probability weighting estimation and its application to predictive covariates — Score 5/6

- **Journal**: Statistical Methods in Medical Research
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-18
- **Authors**: Luo S et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Improve efficiency of IPW estimators of average causal effects by adding constrained outcome working models.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Asymptotic theory, simulation studies, and a diabetes dataset (retinopathy vs HbA1c); sample size not reported.
  - **Estimand / target**: Average causal effect (ATE).
  - **Identification**: Standard propensity-score assumptions; double robustness if either propensity or constrained outcome model is correct.
  - **Estimator / model**: IPW augmented with outcome working models having zero marginal treatment contrast; asymptotic variance no greater than standard AIPW.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Extensive finite-sample simulations comparing adjustment with and without predictive covariates.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Efficiency gain over standard IPW/AIPW when both models correct; strong evidence of retinopathy–HbA1c causal link in application (numbers not reported).
- **Strengths**: Clear theoretical efficiency guarantee with double robustness.
- **Limitations**: Abstract reports no code, numeric simulation results or effect estimates.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Consider efficiency-augmented IPW when predictive covariates are available, rather than plain IPW.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1177/09622802261507295 / PMID:43053468

## 2. Screen device use and body mass development in children: a causal inference approach to sensitive periods — Score 5/6

- **Journal**: International Journal of Epidemiology
- **Pub date**: 2026-10-14
- **Authors**: Koyama Y et al.
- **Type**: applied causal analysis
- **Aims**: Estimate causal effect of screen time on BMI across childhood and test for sensitive periods.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Singapore birth cohort, 1030 children, 10 repeated exposure/outcome assessments, ages 1–10.
  - **Estimand / target**: Effect of 1-SD screen time at each age on subsequent BMI (SD); counterfactual contrasts of early vs sustained interventions.
  - **Identification**: Sequential exchangeability given time-varying confounders affected by prior exposure (feedback).
  - **Estimator / model**: G-estimation of structural nested mean models, contrasted with mixed models.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Comparison with conventional mixed modelling; sex-stratified analyses.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Boys: 0.048-SD BMI increase per 1-SD screen time (95% CI 0.005–0.091), no sensitive period; none in girls.
- **Strengths**: Handles time-varying confounding and exposure-confounder feedback that mixed models cannot.
- **Limitations**: Parent-reported screen time; small effect with wide CI.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Use SNMM g-estimation rather than mixed models to study sensitive periods with repeated exposures.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1093/ije/dyag238 / PMID:43056726

- **Authors**: Samorodnitsky S et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Test association between spatial protein expression and patient-level outcomes without cell segmentation or phenotyping.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Simulations plus triple-negative breast cancer and melanoma immunotherapy spatial proteomics studies; N not reported.
  - **Estimand / target**: Association test (not causal) between topological summaries and patient outcome.
  - **Identification**: Not applicable (association testing).
  - **Estimator / model**: TOASTER: persistent-homology features summarised by Nelson-Aalen-type cumulative hazard; kernel association, functional data, or gridwise testing.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Simulations for power, type I error, and robustness to image gaps/tears.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Improved power with controlled type I error; topological features associated with immunotherapy response.
- **Strengths**: Avoids error-prone segmentation and uses continuous protein levels.
- **Limitations**: Association only; software availability not stated in abstract.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Consider segmentation-free topological tests for spatial biomarker association studies.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1093/biomtc/ujag173 / PMID:43055227

## 5. Not a panacea: the role of presenting descriptive statistics and effect sizes in abstracts on medical students' misconceptions about p-values — Score 3/6

- **Journal**: BMC Medical Research Methodology
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-19
- **Authors**: Eisenhut FS et al.
- **Type**: simulation
- **Aims**: Test whether adding descriptive statistics and effect sizes reduces medical students' p-value misconceptions.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Experiment with 121 medical students reading three fictitious abstracts with varied information sets.
  - **Estimand / target**: Number of endorsed p-value misconceptions and perceived effect size by information set.
  - **Identification**: Randomised information-set variation (allocation details not reported).
  - **Estimator / model**: Group comparisons of misconception counts; model not reported.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Three abstracts varying N, p-value and Cohen's d.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Misconceptions were frequent in all versions; more information reduced them only for large-N, tiny-effect abstract.
- **Strengths**: Directly tests a common reporting recommendation.
- **Limitations**: Small student sample with fictitious abstracts.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Reporting effect sizes alone is insufficient; pair with explicit teaching on interpretation of significance.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-03075-w / PMID:43052765
