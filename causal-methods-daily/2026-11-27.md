## 1. Meta-regression model choice and the interpretation of trial-level surrogacy — Score 5/6

- **Journal**: BMC Medical Research Methodology
- **Pub date**: 2026-09-03
- **Authors**: Collier W et al.
- **Type**: tutorial/guidance
- **Aims**: Compare meta-regression models for trial-level surrogacy and show how their handling of estimation error changes the surrogacy estimand.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Patient-level meta-analysis of 66 chronic kidney disease trials plus a small simulation study.
  - **Estimand / target**: Trial-level R² between true (latent) treatment effects on the clinical and surrogate endpoints.
  - **Identification**: Hierarchical models separate within-trial sampling error from between-trial variation in true effects; OLS/WLS treat estimated effects as if they were the true ones.
  - **Estimator / model**: Unweighted OLS, sample-size-weighted WLS, and bivariate hierarchical meta-regression of treatment effects.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Simulation comparing bias in R² across approaches; empirical comparison on CKD trial collection.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: OLS and WLS gave lower R² than hierarchical models. In simulation they were biased downward for the R² of true effects.
- **Strengths**: Ties model choice explicitly to the estimand, using a large real trial collection.
- **Limitations**: The simulation is small, and the results come from one clinical area (CKD).
- **Takeaway for practice**: When evaluating surrogates, use hierarchical meta-regression that accounts for estimation error, and state the R² estimand explicitly.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-02996-w / PMID:43086702

## 2. Distributed optimal subsampling inference for the additive hazards model with massive survival data — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: BMC Medical Research Methodology
- **Pub date**: 2026-09-04
- **Authors**: Wang W et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Allow privacy-preserving, computationally feasible additive hazards inference across distributed multicentre survival data.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Distributed or multicentre registry survival data; simulations plus an illustrative application.
  - **Estimand / target**: Additive hazards regression coefficients (covariate effects on the hazard difference).
  - **Identification**: Standard additive hazards assumptions with independent censoring; each site subsamples optimally with inverse-probability weights.
  - **Estimator / model**: Optimal-subsampling IPW estimator per site, then divide-and-conquer aggregation of low-dimensional summary statistics at a central server.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Consistency and asymptotic normality proven; simulations vary covariate distributions, censoring rates and heterogeneity between sites.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Stable finite-sample performance across scenarios and clear computational gains in the application. The abstract gives no numeric metrics.
- **Strengths**: Handles privacy constraints and scale together, with theoretical guarantees.
- **Limitations**: Additive hazards only; between-site heterogeneity is handled only in simulation scenarios.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Consider it for federated registry survival analyses where pooling individual data is not allowed.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-02985-z / PMID:43086640

## 3. Variance component score test for multivariate change point detection with applications to mobile health — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: Statistical Methods in Medical Research
- **Pub date**: 2026-11-25
- **Authors**: Martin ML et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Detect shifts in means and/or variances in high-dimensional multivariate time series in real time for mobile-health monitoring.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Simulations plus passively collected smartphone data from adolescents and young adults with affective instability.
  - **Estimand / target**: Presence and timing of a distributional change point across many features.
  - **Identification**: Parameters estimated only from pre-change-point data, so the null distribution is not contaminated by post-change data.
  - **Estimator / model**: Variance component score test (VC*), compared against existing multivariate change point methods.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Simulation of power and of the bias–variance trade-off of using pre-change data only.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: VC* had higher power than competing methods. Using only pre-change data reduced bias by more than it increased variance in most scenarios.
- **Strengths**: Works when features outnumber observations and suits online use.
- **Limitations**: Needs a clean pre-change baseline; the abstract does not quantify false-alarm behaviour.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Useful for triggering just-in-time interventions from digital phenotyping data.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1177/09622802261491950 / PMID:43081806

## 4. Modeling the impact of acute cannabis consumption on pupil light response using nested-crossed function-on-scalar regression — Score 4/6

- **Journal**: Biostatistics
- **Pub date**: 2026-01-20
- **Authors**: Godbole S et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Model pupil light-response curves with nested-crossed repeated structure to detect recent cannabis use.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Handheld pupillometer curves from both eyes at 3 time points; daily, occasional and non-users of cannabis.
  - **Estimand / target**: Fixed-effect functional differences in pupil response curves by user group and time since use.
  - **Identification**: Observational group comparison; nested-crossed functional random effects capture within- and between-curve correlation.
  - **Estimator / model**: Function-on-scalar regression; method-of-moments estimators for the nested-crossed functional covariances; FPCA.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: not reported
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Pupil response differed significantly between users and non-users, and the differences lasted up to 100 minutes after use.
- **Strengths**: Fixed-effect inference stays computationally efficient despite a complex correlation structure.
- **Limitations**: Group contrasts are not causal estimands, and generalisability beyond the study sample is unclear.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Use this approach for repeated functional outcomes measured with crossed factors (eye × visit).
- **ID**: DOI:10.1093/biostatistics/kxag045 / PMID:43079835

## 5. Adaptive Bayesian transfer learning under compositional generalized linear mixed model for microbiome data analysis — Score 3/6

- **Journal**: BMC Medical Research Methodology
- **Pub date**: 2026-09-17
- **Authors**: Luo X et al.
- **Type**: new method
- **Aims**: Improve prediction in small-sample microbiome studies by borrowing information adaptively from related source datasets.
- **Methodology**:
  - **Design & data**: Simulations plus gut microbiome applications: BMI prediction, type 2 diabetes diagnosis and colorectal cancer diagnosis.
  - **Estimand / target**: Predictive performance for the target phenotype; taxa-level compositional coefficients.
  - **Identification**: Power prior with an adaptive transfer parameter that down-weights source data incompatible with the target.
  - **Estimator / model**: Bayesian compositional GLMM (BCGLMM) with phylogenetic structure and power-prior transfer learning.
  - **Sensitivity / validation**: Simulations vary source–target discrepancy, dimension, sample size and covariance structure; compared with target-only, pooled and other transfer-learning methods.
- **Software**: not reported
- **Key findings**: Outperformed target-only, pooled and other transfer methods, especially with small target samples, and stayed stable as source–target discrepancy grew.
- **Strengths**: The adaptive weighting reduces the risk of negative transfer.
- **Limitations**: The focus is prediction, not causal inference; no numeric gains are reported in the abstract.
- **Takeaway for practice**: Consider it for small disease-specific microbiome cohorts when a related larger dataset exists.
- **ID**: DOI:10.1186/s12874-026-03009-6 / PMID:43064939
