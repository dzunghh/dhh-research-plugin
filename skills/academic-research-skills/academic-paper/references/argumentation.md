# Argumentation and Critical Thinking Moves

**Correction to the original plan**: this book is not a formal argumentation/rhetoric text
(thesis–evidence–warrant–rebuttal). It teaches six intellectual strategies used across academic
disciplines — Defining, Summarizing, Serializing, Classifying, Comparing, Analyzing — as the
underlying moves that argument-building draws on. It has no "counterargument" or "rebuttal"
vocabulary; its closest equivalent is **evaluating multiple perspectives** (Ch.6), which is a
different move: juxtaposing readings of the same material rather than raising and answering an
objection to a claim. Use accordingly — don't force this book to supply a formal rebuttal
structure it doesn't contain.

## Thesis construction (Ch.5, Comparing)

A working thesis has to do two things in tension: be **broad enough** to cover everything the
essay actually needs to discuss, and **focused enough** that a reader comes away persuaded of
something specific. The book's own diagnostic: given a set of candidate theses about the same
material, ask which is too broad to organize the specific points already gathered, and which is
too narrow to accommodate them — the workable thesis is the one that exactly spans the argument's
actual scope, not the most dramatic-sounding claim available.

Weak theses tend to fail in one of two directions: description without a claim ("Jefferson and
Hamilton had different views" — true but not yet arguable), or a claim too large for the evidence
in hand ("Hamilton was simply wrong about everything"). A useful pass: state the thesis, then list
what would count as evidence against it — if nothing would, it isn't a claim yet.

## The six strategies, as they feed argument-building

- **Defining** (Ch.1) — pin down a term from context before arguing with it; redefinition
  (arguing that the standard definition is inadequate for the case at hand) is itself a valid
  argumentative move, not just housekeeping.
- **Summarizing** (Ch.2) — of an argument, a table, or a narrative; the discipline of summarizing
  an opposing position accurately, before responding to it, is the book's nearest equivalent to
  fair treatment of a counter-view.
- **Serializing** (Ch.3) — explaining a process or interpreting events/a text sequentially;
  useful for methods sections and for arguments that turn on causal or chronological order.
- **Classifying** (Ch.4) — applying, creating, or reevaluating categories; most literature-review
  and discussion arguments are implicitly classification arguments (these studies group this way,
  and the grouping itself is the claim).
- **Comparing** (Ch.5) — where the thesis-construction guidance above lives; the strongest
  comparative arguments come from one governing idea, not a list of parallel similarities and
  differences with no organizing claim.
- **Analyzing** (Ch.6) — applying a perspective, evaluating a perspective, and evaluating
  *multiple* perspectives against the same material. This is the discipline-relevant move for
  peer review and critique: read the same result through more than one interpretive lens before
  settling on which reading the evidence actually supports, rather than adopting the first
  plausible interpretation.

## Practical use in this skill

- `structure_architect_agent` / `intake_agent`: use the thesis diagnostic above when helping a
  user sharpen a working thesis or research question — check scope-fit, not just clarity.
- `peer_reviewer_agent` / `revision_coach_agent`: "evaluating multiple perspectives" is the
  right frame for asking whether a manuscript's discussion has considered alternative
  interpretations of its own results, distinct from checking whether it cites opposing literature.
- `argument_builder_agent`: this book doesn't supply a rebuttal formalism, so pair it with the
  ordinary academic-writing convention (state the strongest counter-reading, then show why the
  paper's interpretation better fits the evidence) rather than importing a Toulmin-style
  claim-warrant-rebuttal structure that isn't grounded in this canon.
