# Scoping reviews, rapid reviews and other review types

Use this file when the question isn't a standard effectiveness/prognosis SR+MA, or when time or resources force shortcuts. Part A: scoping reviews (JBI method + PRISMA-ScR). Part B: rapid reviews. Part C: other review types.

---

## A. Scoping reviews

### A1. Decide: scoping review or systematic review?

Ask the reviewer what the output is for (JBI 10.1.1). Then:

| Purpose | Choose |
|---|---|
| Feed a clinical guideline, answer a precise clinical question, or inform practice or policy | **Systematic review** |
| Feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness or effectiveness of an intervention | **Systematic review** |
| Map the breadth, volume and types of evidence in a field | Scoping |
| Clarify key concepts, definitions or conceptual boundaries | Scoping |
| Examine how research is done on a topic (designs, measures, tools) | Scoping |
| Identify key characteristics or factors related to a concept | Scoping |
| Identify and analyse knowledge gaps | Scoping |
| Precursor to an SR: is there enough evidence, and what question can it answer? | Scoping |

These are the six JBI indications (Munn 2018, cited in JBI 10.1.1).

Red flags that the user really wants an SR: "does X work", "is X better than Y", a pooled effect estimate, GRADE, or practice recommendations. Say so and redirect to the SR workflow.

How a scoping review differs from an SR (JBI 10.1.2, Table 11.1):
- Same: a priori protocol; explicit, transparent search; standardised charting forms.
- Different: critical appraisal is optional, not mandatory. No synthesised summary findings. Meta-analysis and meta-synthesis are typically not done.
- A scoping review is **not** a mixed-methods SR. A scoping review maps which kinds of evidence exist. A mixed-methods SR answers a question by synthesising qualitative and quantitative evidence together (JBI 10.1.2).
- The JBI framework builds on Arksey & O'Malley (2005) and Levac et al. (2010). Peters et al. extend it to nine stages (JBI 10.1.3, Table 11.2):
  1. Objectives and questions.
  2. Inclusion criteria.
  3. Planned methods.
  4. Search.
  5. Selection.
  6. Extraction.
  7. Analysis.
  8. Presentation.
  9. Summary and implications.
- Stakeholder and librarian consultation runs through all nine stages.

### A2. Question framing – PCC

Use **Population, Concept, Context**, not PICO (JBI 10.2.2, 10.2.4).
- **Population** – the key traits and qualifying criteria, such as age. Population can be dropped when it isn't relevant, for example when mapping research designs.
- **Concept** – the core thing being mapped. It may include interventions, phenomena of interest or outcomes, but these need not be explicit.
- **Context** – geographic, cultural, social or gender factors, or a setting such as acute, primary or community care. Context can be left "open".
- **Types of evidence sources** – may be left open: primary studies, reviews, guidelines, letters, websites, policy documents. Justify any limits.
- **Title** – ends with ": a scoping review" (or "…protocol"). Don't phrase it as a question, and keep it to 25 words or fewer (JBI 10.3.1).
- **Questions** – one primary question. Add sub-questions only when they show how the map will be split, for example by population age band or by setting.
- Keep the title, question and inclusion criteria congruent. Check all three against each other before finalising the protocol.

### A3. Protocol and registration

- An a priori protocol is mandatory. Highlight and justify any deviations in the final report (JBI 10.2). Some iteration is expected in a scoping review, but it must still be documented (JBI 10.3).
- **PROSPERO does not accept scoping reviews.** Register on **OSF** (osf.io) or Figshare, or publish the protocol in a journal such as JBI Evidence Synthesis (JBI 10.2).
- JBI protocol template elements (JBI 10.2.1–10.2.9):
  - Title (PCC-structured).
  - Review question and sub-questions.
  - Introduction of about 1,000 words. It states the rationale for a *scoping* approach, gives definitions, and reports a preliminary search for existing scoping reviews and SRs: which sources were searched (e.g. JBI Evidence Synthesis, CDSR, Epistemonikos, PubMed) and on what dates. If a review already exists, it explains how this one differs.
  - Inclusion criteria: participants, concept, context, types of evidence sources.
  - Search strategy (A4).
  - Source selection process (A5).
  - Draft data-charting form (A6).
  - Planned analysis (A7).
  - Planned presentation of results – a draft table or figure (A7).
  - Appendix: the full search strategy for at least one database.

### A4. Search

- Aim for as comprehensive a search as time and resources allow, published **and** grey literature. State and justify any limits on breadth (JBI 10.2.5).
- Use the JBI **three-step search** (JBI 10.2.5):
  1. Run a limited search in at least two relevant databases, e.g. MEDLINE and CINAHL. Mine the titles, abstracts and index terms of the results for search terms.
  2. Run the full search, using every identified keyword and index term, across all chosen databases.
  3. Search the reference lists of identified sources. State whether this covers all sources or only included ones.
- The search is allowed to be **iterative**: new terms and sources can be added as the team learns the field. Every iteration must stay transparent and auditable, so log each revision with its date.
- One broad search across all evidence types at once is acceptable and increases sensitivity (JBI 10.2.5).
- Language: JBI strongly recommends no language restriction unless there is a clear, feasibility-based reason. Justify the date range (JBI 10.2.5).
- Have a librarian build the search and a second librarian peer-review it using PRESS (McGowan 2016, cited in JBI 10.2.5).
- Say whether authors will be contacted. Report hand-searched journals and years, and the results of author contact (JBI 10.3.7.1).
- Reporting: list all information sources with dates of coverage and the date of the last search (PRISMA-ScR item 7). Give the full strategy, with limits, for at least one database (item 8).

### A5. Screening and selection

- Screen in **two stages**: title and abstract, then full text. Two or more reviewers screen independently. Resolve disagreements by consensus or a third reviewer (JBI 10.2.6).
- **Pilot first** (JBI 10.2.6):
  1. The whole team screens a random sample of 25 titles and abstracts.
  2. The team discusses discrepancies and refines the criteria and their elaboration document.
  3. Start full screening only once agreement reaches 75% or more.
- Name the software used, such as Covidence, EndNote or JBI SUMARI.
- Keep appendices listing included sources, and excluded full texts with reasons.
- Report the process in the review (PRISMA-ScR item 9). Report counts at each stage, ideally as a flow diagram (item 14).

### A6. Data charting (extraction)

- In a scoping review, extraction is called **charting** (JBI 10.2.7; PRISMA-ScR footnote).
- Draft the charting form at protocol stage. Typical fields:
  - Author(s) and year.
  - Country of origin.
  - Aims.
  - Population and sample size.
  - Methodology.
  - Intervention and comparator, with details such as duration.
  - Outcomes and how they were measured.
  - Key findings relevant to the review question.
- Start from JBI Appendix 10.1, the template charting instrument, and cite JBI if you adapt it.
- **Refine iteratively.** Add fields if new, useful data emerge during charting, and record every change. Pilot the form on 2–3 sources with at least two reviewers (JBI 10.2.7).
- Duplicate charting is preferred. One extractor with a second verifier is acceptable if dual charting isn't feasible – state which was used (JBI 10.2.7; PRISMA-ScR item 10).
- Define every charted variable and any assumptions or simplifications (PRISMA-ScR item 11).

### A7. Critical appraisal, analysis and presentation

**Critical appraisal is generally not done** (JBI 10.1.1, Table 11.1). It is optional when the aim specifically needs it, for example mapping the quality of an evidence base. If it is done, give the rationale and the method, and say how the results were used (PRISMA-ScR items 12 and 16).

Without appraisal, these limits follow:
- No certainty or GRADE rating is possible.
- Implications for practice or policy cannot be graded. They are usually omitted, or are tentative and must flow directly from the objectives (JBI 10.1.2, 10.3.9, 10.3.10).
- Implications for research are the normal output: gaps, and future SR or primary-study questions.
- In the discussion, flag that conclusions describe how much evidence exists and what kind, **not** what works.

**Analysis** (JBI 10.2.8):
- Do not synthesise results the way an SR does. **No meta-analysis**, and no meta-aggregation or meta-ethnography (JBI 10.2.8, Table 11.1). If the user asks for a pooled estimate, stop and recommend an SR instead.
- The default is descriptive frequency counts of concepts, populations, designs, countries and years.
- Optional: basic descriptive qualitative content analysis, or coding against an a priori framework (framework-synthesis style). Keep it descriptive rather than interpretive thematic synthesis.
- Results may be charted descriptively, without assessing their certainty.
- Pre-specify the analysis in the protocol and report it transparently (PRISMA-ScR item 13).

**Presentation** (JBI 10.2.9, 10.3.7.4, 10.3.8):
- Map the results in tables or diagrams, organised by the PCC elements. Examples:
  - Counts by study design.
  - Counts by population age band.
  - Counts by domain.
  - Distribution by year or country.
  - Area of intervention.
- Possible charts: evidence maps or matrices (concept × population, with gaps visible as empty cells), bubble charts of volume by year, and relational diagrams linking strategies, barriers and outcomes.
- Always pair each table or chart with a descriptive narrative that links it back to the question.
- Put the table of characteristics of included sources in an appendix (PRISMA-ScR items 15 and 17).
- In R: use `dplyr::count()` to make the frequency tables and `ggplot2` with `geom_tile()` or `geom_point(aes(size = n))` for evidence or bubble maps.

### A8. PRISMA flow diagram

The PRISMA flow diagram is still required. JBI uses a version adapted from PRISMA (JBI 10.3.8.1, Fig. 11.5; PRISMA-ScR item 14). Generate it with `scripts/prisma_flow.R` and record:
- Records from databases and from other sources, such as grey literature and reference lists.
- Duplicates removed.
- Records screened and excluded.
- Full texts sought and retrieved.
- Full texts excluded, with reasons.
- Additions from the third search step.
- Sources included.

Label the boxes "sources of evidence" rather than "studies".

### A9. PRISMA-ScR – summary (Tricco et al. 2018, Ann Intern Med)

PRISMA-ScR has **22 items: 20 essential and 2 optional**. The optional pair is critical appraisal: item 12 for methods and item 16 for results. Paraphrased:

| # | Section | Report |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | Identify the report as a scoping review |
| 2 | Abstract | Structured summary: background, objectives, eligibility, sources, charting methods, results, conclusions |
| 3 | Rationale | What is already known, and why the question suits a *scoping* approach |
| 4 | Objectives | Explicit questions or objectives with their key elements (e.g. PCC) |
| 5 | Protocol/registration | Whether a protocol exists, where to find it, and the registration number |
| 6 | Eligibility | Source characteristics (years, language, publication status) with rationale |
| 7 | Information sources | All sources, with coverage dates and the date of the last search |
| 8 | Search | Full strategy for at least 1 database, including limits |
| 9 | Selection | Screening and eligibility process |
| 10 | Charting process | Forms (calibrated or piloted), independent or duplicate charting, confirming data with investigators |
| 11 | Data items | Define the variables, plus assumptions and simplifications |
| 12 | Critical appraisal *(optional)* | If done: rationale, methods, and how the results were used |
| 13 | Synthesis | How the charted data were handled and summarised |
| 14 | Selection results | Numbers screened, assessed and included, with reasons for exclusion – ideally a flow diagram |
| 15 | Source characteristics | Charted characteristics and citation for each source |
| 16 | Appraisal results *(optional)* | If done, the appraisal data |
| 17 | Individual results | Charted data relevant to the questions, per source |
| 18 | Synthesis of results | Charting results summarised against the questions |
| 19 | Summary of evidence | Main results (concepts, themes, evidence types), linked to the objectives, with relevance to key groups |
| 20 | Limitations | Limitations of the scoping *process* |
| 21 | Conclusions | General interpretation, implications and next steps |
| 22 | Funding | Funding of the included sources **and** of the review, and the funders' role |

**How PRISMA-ScR differs from PRISMA 2020:**
- It uses "sources of evidence" (studies, opinion, policy documents) instead of "studies". It uses "critical appraisal" instead of risk of bias, and "charting" instead of data extraction.
- There are no items on effect measures, synthesis methods for pooling, heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis, reporting bias or certainty of evidence (GRADE). These are all core to PRISMA 2020.
- Critical appraisal is optional (items 12 and 16). In PRISMA 2020 risk of bias is mandatory.
- The rationale must justify the scoping approach itself (item 3).
- The funding item also covers the funding of the included sources (item 22).
- PRISMA-ScR is based on the 2009 PRISMA. Many authors now use the PRISMA 2020 flow-diagram layout – state which one you used.
- JBI provides a fillable PRISMA-ScR checklist in Appendix 10.2.

## B. Rapid reviews

### B1. Definition

There is no single agreed definition. Hamel et al. (2021, J Clin Epidemiol) analysed definitions from 216 rapid reviews and 90 methods papers and found eight themes:
1. Accelerated process.
2. Varied methods shortcuts.
3. Focus or breadth of scope.
4. Contrast with a full SR.
5. Stakeholder rationale.
6. Resource efficiency.
7. Remains systematic.
8. Bias and limitations.

Hamel et al. suggest a working definition, paraphrased: a rapid review is a knowledge synthesis that speeds up the SR process by streamlining or omitting selected methods, so that evidence is produced resource-efficiently. They also give optional versions that add the stakeholder theme or the rigour and transparency theme.

Other labels include rapid evidence assessment (REA, used by UK government), rapid systematic review and restricted review. Define the methods rather than relying on the label (Hamel 2021).

Use a rapid review when a decision-maker needs evidence urgently, typically on a new or emerging topic (Temple; Plymouth REA page). Don't use one when stakes need full certainty, such as a guideline or regulatory decision with no deadline.

### B2. Acceptable streamlining

**Flag:** the specifics below come from Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group (RRMG) guidance (Garritty et al. 2021 interim guidance; 2024 update, J Clin Epidemiol). They are drawn from **general knowledge, not from the local source files**. Check the current version before citing.

| Step | Acceptable shortcut | Minimum safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Narrow the question; limit the outcomes, e.g. to the most important ones for decisions | Agree the scope with the commissioner or stakeholders up front |
| Protocol | Brief protocol | Still write one and register it (PROSPERO accepts rapid reviews of health outcomes; otherwise OSF) |
| Databases | Fewer databases, e.g. MEDLINE, Embase and CENTRAL; limited grey literature | Search peer-reviewed by a librarian (PRESS) if possible |
| Limits | Date limits; English-only unless there is a reason otherwise | Justify each limit |
| Existing reviews | Use existing SRs as the starting point or as a source of studies | Check their currency and quality |
| T/A screening | Two reviewers dual-screen a calibration sample (about 20%), then one reviewer screens the rest | A second reviewer checks the excluded records |
| Full-text screening | One reviewer | A second reviewer checks the exclusions |
| Extraction | One extractor, with a limited set of data items | A second reviewer verifies the extraction for accuracy and completeness |
| Risk of bias | One assessor using a validated tool, limited to key outcomes | A second reviewer verifies it |
| Synthesis | Narrative synthesis by default; meta-analysis only if appropriate | Follow SWiM for narrative synthesis |
| Certainty | GRADE for key outcomes only | One rater, with verification |

Plymouth (REA page) lists similar trade-offs: limited databases, restricted study types, less attention to quality, limited extraction and a smaller team.

### B3. What must not be cut

- An explicit, reproducible search with a documented strategy.
- Pre-stated eligibility criteria and a written protocol.
- Some second-person verification of single-reviewer steps: screening exclusions, extraction and risk of bias.
- Risk-of-bias assessment of included studies. It may be limited in scope, but don't drop it if conclusions are meant to inform decisions.
- Transparent reporting of every shortcut.
- Stakeholder engagement on scope and deadline.
- Honest conclusions that are proportionate to the certainty of the evidence.

### B4. Reporting

- Report against PRISMA 2020 until PRISMA-RR is published (it is in development – general knowledge; verify status). Include the flow diagram from `scripts/prisma_flow.R`.
- Title and abstract: identify the report as a rapid review.
- Methods: list every shortcut explicitly, ideally in a table of each step against the full-SR standard and the shortcut taken.
- Limitations: discuss each shortcut as a potential source of bias, such as missed studies or extraction error. Say which direction the bias would push the results, where you can (Hamel 2021, theme 8).

### B5. Timeline

- Weeks to a few months. The Cochrane RRMG typically targets **under 6 months** (general knowledge).
- The original 1997 UK rapid HTA service produced reports within about two person-months (Hamel 2021).
- Plymouth describes REAs as done by a smaller team over several weeks.
- Agree the deadline with the commissioner first, then pick the shortcuts that fit it.

## C. Other review types – quick routing

| Type | When | Method / reporting to follow |
|---|---|---|
| **Umbrella review / overview of reviews** | Many SRs already exist on a topic; synthesise across reviews for decision-makers | JBI Manual ch. 9 (umbrella reviews); Cochrane Handbook ch. V (overviews); report with PRIOR; appraise reviews with AMSTAR 2 or ROBIS; handle overlapping primary studies (CCA) |
| **Mixed-methods SR** | The question needs integrated qualitative and quantitative evidence, e.g. effectiveness plus acceptability | JBI Manual ch. 8 (convergent segregated or integrated approach); PRISMA 2020 |
| **Qualitative evidence synthesis** | Experiences, perceptions, barriers or facilitators | JBI Manual ch. 3 (meta-aggregation, ConQual); Cochrane QES guidance (thematic synthesis or meta-ethnography, GRADE-CERQual); report with ENTREQ (eMERGe for meta-ethnography) |
| **Realist review** | Complex interventions: what works, for whom, in what circumstances and how (context–mechanism–outcome) | Pawson's realist synthesis; RAMESES publication standards (general knowledge) |
| **Mapping review / evidence-gap map** | A large, diverse literature; give a visual map of where evidence exists and where it is missing, to direct future research | Campbell Collaboration EGM guidance (general knowledge). Overlaps with scoping reviews – if published as a scoping review, use JBI 10 and PRISMA-ScR. Usually no synthesis (Plymouth; Temple) |
| **Living systematic review** | A high-priority question with ongoing uncertainty and a fast-moving evidence base | Cochrane living SR guidance (Temple); continual search and update cycles; PRISMA 2020 plus a statement of the living-mode schedule; needs a long-term team commitment |
| **Rapid review** | Urgent decision-making need | Part B |
| **Scoping review** | Map concepts, evidence types and gaps | Part A |
| **Integrative review** | Nursing-style synthesis of empirical and theoretical literature | Whittemore & Knafl methods (general knowledge; Temple names no formal guideline) |
